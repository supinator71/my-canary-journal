## About The Clinical Lens

Some people have a doctor in the family. Most people don't.

That turns out to matter more than almost anything else, and nobody designed it
that way. It's luck. When something goes wrong, one family has somebody who knows
which question to ask and who to ask it of, and the family across the street has
a folder of printouts and a search engine that tells them they're dying.

I'm my mother's doctor in the family. Not officially — officially I'm her
daughter — but I'm the one who keeps track of what she's taking and why, who
notices when something doesn't add up, who makes the call. I'm glad to do it.
She's my mother.

But I think about the version where I'm not here. Or the version where I became
the schoolteacher instead, and never learned any of this. Who does it then?

That question is what this newsletter is for.

### What I actually do

I'm a board-certified psychiatrist. Twenty-plus years, most of it with the people
this system holds onto worst — incarcerated patients, the severely and
chronically ill, the ones who arrived already written off by somebody.

The most durable thing I learned in all of it is that outcomes were almost never
decided by the diagnosis. They were decided by whether a person had a bed, a door
that locked, food, and someone who believed them.

**Health is housing. Health is safety. Health is being believed.**

I write here about serious illness — cancer in particular — and about the two
questions people ask most and get answered least: *what is the next step,* and
*what are the chances.* How the machine works. What the words mean. What to ask,
and who to ask.

Let me be precise about what I am and am not, because it matters. I'm a general
psychiatrist. I'm not an oncologist and I'm not a psycho-oncologist — those are
real subspecialties with real training that I don't have. What I have is two
decades of watching what serious illness does to a nervous system and to a
family, years of working alongside oncologists and reading everything I could,
and a long history of being the person friends and family call when someone they
love gets a phone call from a doctor.

On the medicine itself, I'll show you my sources. On what fear and waiting and
not being believed do to a human being, I'm telling you what I know.

Cancer runs in my family. I've been in the waiting room and I've been the patient
in the bed. That's the whole of my personal disclosure, and I'm not going to make
you read a memoir.

### What this is not

I'm a doctor. **I'm not your doctor.** Nothing here is a treatment plan for you or
anyone you love. This is general education — how the system works, what to ask,
what to expect — and the most useful thing I can usually give you is a better
question to bring to your own team.

I also don't care who you vote for. Whatever you believe, whoever you supported,
you deserve to understand what's happening inside your own body and to get the
care you need. This isn't that kind of newsletter and it isn't going to become
one.

Send me questions. I'll answer the general question underneath the specific one —
publicly, and without ever publishing your details.

It's free. All of it, always. I'm not here to gate this, I'm here to reach people.

*The views here are mine and not any employer's. If you're in crisis, call or
text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.*

> Board-certified psychiatrist, 20+ years. I write about serious illness and how
> to get through the system that treats it — what the next step is, what the
> chances are, and what to ask. Health is housing. Health is safety. Health is
> being believed.

**Medium (masthead):**
> A psychiatrist on serious illness, and how to get a system that wasn't built to
> answer you to answer you anyway.

**Short (one-liner):**
> Some people have a doctor in the family. Consider me yours.

*(That last one is the most likable and the most likely to overpromise — it
edges toward the individual-advice line you drew. Use it only where there's
room for the "I'm not your doctor" disclaimer nearby, or not at all.)*
