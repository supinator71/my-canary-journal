# I Was Checking His Blood for a Side Effect. It Was Leukemia.

*A routine lab caught a young man's cancer. Watching Nathan Fielder's new Elizabeth Holmes documentary, I kept thinking about what happens when the number is a lie.*

---

Every psychiatrist learns to watch the blood. It is one of the quieter habits of the job. When you start someone on an antipsychotic, you draw labs. You check the metabolic numbers, because these medications can nudge blood sugar and cholesterol in the wrong direction and pile on weight over time. I keep an eye on the white blood cells as well, because, [rarely](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9902410/), these drugs can pull down a person's infection-fighting cells. It is a small risk. Most months, most years, the labs come back and tell you nothing you did not already expect. You order them anyway. That is the whole point of watching: you watch when nothing is happening, so that you are still watching on the day something is.

He was in his early twenties. I was working at a community mental health agency then. He had come to us after a hospitalization, in the middle of what we call a first episode of psychosis. That is a frightening, disorienting stretch when a young person's mind begins to work against them for the first time, and no one, least of all the young person, yet knows what it means or where it is going. He had been started on quetiapine in the hospital. My job was to help him find his footing: steady the symptoms, manage the side effects, and help him and his family understand what was happening and what came next.

He was sharp. That is the thing I keep coming back to. Psychosis does not make a person less intelligent, and it had not made him less intelligent. He asked good questions. He noticed things. He was the kind of young man who, under different circumstances, you could imagine arguing with in a seminar.

So I did what I do. I drew his labs. His metabolic numbers had started to drift, which we addressed, and that was its own small victory. What caught my eye, though, was a dip in his absolute neutrophil count, the infection-fighting white cells. It was slight. Nine times out of ten, a mild dip like that on quetiapine is nothing: a transient wobble, a harmless baseline some people are simply born with, the ordinary physiological noise of a human body. The careful move is neither to panic nor to ignore it. The careful move is to wait a couple of months and draw it again. So that is what I did.

When the second set of labs came back, with him sitting across from me, his white blood cell count was not low. It was high. High in a way that stops your breath for a second: 18.4, when normal tops out around 11. I remember looking at the number and feeling the floor of the conversation shift under me, because this was no longer a question about a medication side effect. This was a question about why a young man's bone marrow was suddenly producing white cells like that.

People sometimes forget that psychiatrists went to medical school first. When a lab like that lands on your screen in a community mental health clinic, the subspecialty walls disappear and you go straight back to being a general physician. I talked to him plainly about how the marrow makes those cells, and why a jump like that meant someone needed to look closely at his blood right away.

I did not send him home with a referral slip. I picked up the phone while he was still sitting across from me and called his primary care doctor's office. By some small miracle of clinical timing, I actually got his doctor on the line. I explained the numbers, and we arranged for him to be seen the next day.

I was moving quickly. He could see that I was moving quickly, and he was far too smart to miss what that meant.

He looked at me, steady and frightened, and asked the question I have never forgotten.

"Do I have cancer?"

I will not pretend I had a perfect answer. You do not get to lie to a person like that, and you should not want to. I told him the truth, which was that I did not know yet. I told him the number was something we had to take seriously right away, and that I would make sure he was not alone in chasing it down. Whatever it was, we were going to find out fast, and finding out fast was the best thing we could do.

He did have cancer. It was [acute lymphoblastic leukemia](https://www.cancer.gov/types/leukemia/patient/adult-all-treatment-pdq). It was caught because I had been watching a part of his blood for a reason that had nothing to do with the disease that was actually there.

What stayed with me for years afterward was what happened next. He started chemotherapy, and he kept coming to see me at the agency. Month after month, he kept improving on both fronts, holding on to his psychiatric stability right through leukemia treatment. Eventually I left for another job, and I lost track of him, which is one of the quietest griefs in medicine.

I think about him often. I think about the strange grace of it: that a lab I drew out of ordinary caution, for a side effect that turned out not to matter, is the reason we found the thing that did. And I think about his question, asked plainly, without flinching, by a young man who deserved the plain answer I tried to give him.

## The analyzer on the dining table

I thought about him this week while watching [*You Can See Everything*](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2026/oct/01/you-can-see-everything-movie-review), the new Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim documentary, in theaters now. It follows Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes through the thirty-four days before she reported to federal prison. Most of the talk about the film is about its hall of mirrors, and I'll leave its surprises for you to find.

The moment that stayed with me was a quieter one. Early in the film, Fielder and Holmes sit at the dinner table of her beach rental. Spread out between them are the disassembled insides of the Theranos blood analyzer, which [she still hopes](https://www.salon.com/2026/10/17/nathan-fielders-you-can-see-everything-and-the-tragedy-of-elizabeth-holmes/) to turn into a new product someday. She talks about how her technology was misunderstood. The metal and plastic pieces sit there like props from a magic show.

I am not going to tell you what is going on inside Elizabeth Holmes. I have not examined her, and I don't diagnose people from documentaries. My question was simpler, the one I would want a patient to ask: how did that machine get from a Palo Alto lab into about forty Walgreens stores?

In an exam room, a blood test is a load-bearing wall. Everything else is built on the assumption that the number is real.

## How a blood test gets to the pharmacy counter

Most people assume that before a company can draw their blood at a pharmacy, the FDA has confirmed the machine works. For many tests, that is not what happens. The drugs I prescribe have to clear years of trials. Tests designed and run inside a single lab, called [laboratory-developed tests](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/in-vitro-diagnostics/laboratory-developed-tests), have mostly lived outside FDA review since the 1970s, on the theory that they were small-volume tests made by local labs for local needs. Theranos ran most of its menu that way, inside its own certified lab, and [never published peer-reviewed research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25688775/) describing how its technology worked.

The retailers were the other possible check. Safeway [spent roughly $350 million](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/medical-devices/safeway-severs-ties-theranos-as-350m-deal-collapses) building clinic space for Theranos in hundreds of its stores; Theranos missed its deadlines, and the testing never launched. Walgreens did launch, in about forty stores, nearly all in Arizona, and [according to later reporting](https://fortune.com/2016/05/26/walgreens-didnt-verify-theranos/), never independently tested the devices first. Arizona even passed a 2015 law, [which Theranos lobbied for](https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/news/author-john-carreyrou-how-theranos-scammed-arizona-10477429/), letting people order their own blood tests without a doctor.

I don't think the lesson is that one company lied, though it did. The regulator deferred to the lab's certification. The lab belonged to the company. The pharmacy leaned on the company's word. The customer leaned on the pharmacy's name over the door. Each layer assumed another layer had done the checking. It was a misallocation of trust, and nobody was standing where the blood was.

And the blood is unforgiving. When you [squeeze a fingertip](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK138654/) for a few drops, you can rupture red cells, which [throws off potassium](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009571/), and dilute the sample with tissue fluid. Even [drop to drop](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26572995/), fingerstick white counts vary far more than blood from a vein, which is why most blood counts come from your arm. Theranos eventually [voided two years of results](https://techcrunch.com/2016/05/18/theranos-voids-tests/) from its own device and issued tens of thousands of corrected reports. A woman in Arizona was [told by a Theranos result that her pregnancy was failing](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/09/21/theranos-test-gave-false-miscarriage-diagnosis-witness-testifies.html). It wasn't.

## When the number lies

Go back to that clinic and imagine he had been sent to a pharmacy counter running unvalidated fingerstick tests. There are two ways a bad lab hurts someone in that chair, and both are quiet.

The first is that it misses the signal. As leukemia crowds the marrow, [normal blood counts can fall](https://www.cancer.gov/types/leukemia/hp/adult-all-treatment-pdq). A noisy test can turn a real dip into a normal-looking number, or dilute a white count of 18.4 into something that alarms no one. Then there is no phone call that afternoon. And when a young man with a psychiatric diagnosis later shows up at urgent care, exhausted and feverish, there is a name for what can happen next: [diagnostic overshadowing](https://doi.org/10.1111/jocn.16151), when physical symptoms get attributed to mental illness. Without an accurate number to force the issue, he goes home.

The second is that it breaks the trust that lets a physician act in real time. When I saw 18.4, I did not spend two weeks wondering whether a glitchy machine had made it up. I picked up the phone with him still in the room. Moving that fast depends on a number you can trust.

## The door is still open

It is tempting to file Theranos under closed cases. Holmes is in federal prison; the company is gone. The gap it used is not. In May 2024, the FDA issued a rule to regulate laboratory-developed tests as medical devices. In March 2025, a federal court ruled that the agency lacked the authority and struck it down, and in September 2025 the FDA [restored the old text](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/in-vitro-diagnostics/laboratory-developed-tests). Reasonable people disagree about whether the FDA is the right referee. For a patient holding a result, the upshot is simpler: many tests reach you without the kind of federal review your medications get.

## What to do with a number

None of this is a reason to distrust the blood work your own doctor orders. Most labs, on most days, do careful work. It is a reason to keep a few habits, the kind a family with a doctor in it uses without thinking.

**Treat one surprising number as a reason to recheck, not to panic and not to ignore.** That was the whole move with his first dip. Clinicians read trends, so ask: "What does this mean for what we do next?"

**If you bought the test yourself, online, at a pharmacy, or in a wellness panel, bring the result to a clinician who knows you** before anyone acts on it, and ask whether it should be repeated at a standard lab.

**Ask how you'll hear.** "Will results come through the portal, or will someone call? What happens if something is off?" Write the answer down.

I've put these and a few more on a one-page printable: [**The Corner Kit: Lab Tests**](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1rHqyTwxTqygrkZ3PeywAy9M5mWIO4Tw6/view). Half of it is for patients and families, and half is for the clinicians, nurses, and staff who read results and pass them on. It's free. Print it, put it on the fridge, or hand it to whoever is the point person in your family.

Watching Elizabeth Holmes across the table from Nathan Fielder, over the gutted pieces of her analyzer, it would be easy to treat the whole thing as surreal comedy. I can't quite laugh. Every time I see that little black box, I think about a young man sitting across from me in a community mental health clinic, steady and frightened, asking me if he had cancer.

We watch the blood when nothing is happening, so that we are still watching on the day something is. For that to mean anything, the blood test has to tell the truth.
