# They Called Me Because She Was Anxious. She Hadn't Eaten in Two Days.

*DRAFT 3 — no placeholders left. Ready to read as a finished piece.*

---

Every psychiatrist does a year of general medicine before they get to be a
psychiatrist. One year on the medical units, then three years of psychiatry
after it. Intern year.

Mine was, honestly, a pleasure. I spent it with the medicine interns and the
specialty fellows, and I liked them, and I liked the work. It was tiring in the
way that year is tiring for everyone. It was also where a disproportionate number
of the things I actually know came from, which I did not appreciate at the time.

The consult came in first thing in the morning. A woman on the urology service —
sixty-five, maybe sixty. Very slight. She was in for a procedure, she had a
documented history of anxiety and depression, and the team had seen her on
rounds and noted that she seemed anxious. So they called psychiatry, which is
what you do.

Nothing about this was unusual. That's the part worth holding onto.

I went up. I sat down. I asked her how she was doing.

She told me she hadn't eaten in two days.

I want to be precise about what happened next in my own head, because it is the
whole reason I am writing this. What happened was not insight. It was a kind of
stall — the sensation of a sentence arriving that does not fit the category you
were sent up there to fill. I had been called about anxiety. I was holding a
consult request about anxiety. And the patient had just told me, without any
particular drama, that she was hungry.

Nobody had ordered her food.

Not as a clinical decision. Not NPO for a procedure, held for a reason, with a
plan to resume. Her diet order had simply never been entered, and then it had
gone on not being entered, across two days and several shift changes and an
unknown number of people walking past the foot of her bed.

I called urology. I asked them to order her a tray. They did. She ate.

And then she felt better — not metaphorically, not eventually. She felt better.
The anxiety that had generated a psychiatric consultation resolved substantially
in response to a sandwich.

## What her nervous system was actually doing

Here is the part that has stayed with me for twenty years, and it is the part
that turns this from an anecdote into something you can use.

She was not misdiagnosed because anyone was stupid. She was displaying, quite
accurately, what a human nervous system does when it is running on nothing.

Sustained caloric deprivation is a physiological stressor, and the body responds
to physiological stressors with the machinery it has: catecholamines, cortisol,
a sympathetic system that shifts toward vigilance. Blood sugar drops and
adrenaline rises to compensate. The result — from the inside — is a racing
heart, a jittery restlessness, a sense of dread with no available object, and
difficulty concentrating on anything that is not immediate.

That is also, more or less exactly, what a panic state feels like. The body does
not have a separate signal for *I am frightened* and *I have not eaten.* It has
one alarm, and it rings the same way regardless of what pulled the cord.

So a woman lying in a hospital bed running on two days of nothing will look
anxious. She will *be* anxious, in the sense that her nervous system is doing
the thing anxiety is. And if there happens to be a line in her chart that says
*history of anxiety and depression*, then a story is available, ready-made, that
explains what everyone is seeing without requiring anyone to ask a further
question.

That line in her chart did not help her. It cost her two days.

## Nobody's job

I want to be careful here, because the easy version of this story is the one
where somebody is the villain, and that version is both more satisfying and
less true.

The urology team was not indifferent. They were managing a surgical problem, and
they managed it. The nurses were not negligent; they were carrying a full
assignment and a diet order is not a thing you see, it is a thing you would have
to go looking for. The consulting psychiatrist — me — was called to evaluate
anxiety, and evaluating anxiety was, in the narrowest sense, exactly what I did.

Every single person did their piece correctly.

The problem is that **nobody's piece was the whole woman.** The system had
divided her into a urologic problem, a psychiatric problem, and a set of nursing
tasks, and it had assigned each fragment to someone competent. What it had not
assigned to anyone was the job of noticing that she had not eaten since Tuesday.

That is not a failure of people. It's a failure of what gets valued. Time spent
looking at the whole person is the least reimbursed, least documented, least
protected thing in American medicine. It is nobody's billable hour. So it
reliably becomes nobody's job, and it goes undone in exactly the way you'd
predict, and then a sixty-five-year-old woman is lying in a bed being anxious at
everyone for a reason that a sandwich would fix.

I would like to tell you this was a memorable exception. It happened more than
once.

## Why I am telling you this

This is the last essay of the version of this newsletter you subscribed to, and
the first one of what it is going to be from here.

I have spent twenty years as a psychiatrist, most of it with the people the
system had the hardest time holding onto — incarcerated patients, the severely
and chronically ill, the ones who arrived already written off. And the single
most durable thing I learned is that outcomes were almost never decided by the
diagnosis. They were decided by whether somebody had a bed, a door that locked,
food, and someone who believed them.

Health is housing. Health is safety. Health is being believed.

I am going to be writing that idea in one specific place now: **cancer.** How to
get through a diagnosis, a treatment, and the sprawling logistical catastrophe
that comes with both — for patients, and for the people who love them.

Not because I am an oncologist. I am not, and I will be clear about that every
time it matters. I am a board-certified general psychiatrist who has spent two
decades watching what serious illness does to a nervous system and to a family,
and who has spent roughly as long being the person friends and family call when
somebody they love gets a phone call from a doctor. Cancer runs in my family. I
have been in the waiting room and I have been the patient in the bed. That is
the whole of my personal disclosure, and I am not going to make you read a
memoir.

What I can do is the thing I did in that hospital room. Not treat you — I'm a
doctor, I'm not your doctor, and that distinction is going to be load-bearing
around here. What I can do is tell you how the machine actually works, what the
words mean, which question to ask, and who to ask it of.

## What to do with this one

Two things, and they're both usable this week.

**If someone you love is in a hospital bed and seems anxious, agitated, or
"not themselves" — check the boring things first.** Have they eaten? Have they
slept? Are they in pain that isn't being treated? Do they need the bathroom and
can't reach anyone? Are they wearing their glasses, their hearing aids, their
dentures? A distressing amount of hospital psychiatry is, on inspection, unmet
physical need with a psychiatric label sitting on top of it. This is *more* true,
not less, for anyone who has a mental health history in their chart, because the
history supplies an explanation that stops the questions.

**And know that you may have to be the one who notices.** Not because the staff
are bad — they are, overwhelmingly, good — but because the system has genuinely
not assigned that job to anyone, and you are the only person in the building
whose entire portfolio is this one human being. That's not an adversarial
posture. It's a role. You're allowed to say: *she hasn't eaten since Tuesday,
can someone order a tray.*

Sometimes that is the whole intervention.
