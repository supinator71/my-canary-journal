# Voice & Craft Notes

Close reading of the archive, 2026-07-24. Use this when drafting or editing in
her voice — it records what to reproduce and what to avoid.

## Strengths — reproduce these

**1. Opening images.** Her best asset by a distance. Concrete, closely observed,
faintly absurd, never decorative:
- "deer that eat our plants and lay around on the property like we're the ones renting"
- "herons argue over territory in the water below, which is a dynamic I find professionally familiar"
- "the Steller's jays being outraged about something, which is their default setting"

**2. The clinical aside as punchline.** Her signature move and a genuinely rare
one: a plain observation, then a subordinate clause that reframes it through the
professional lens. "…which is a dynamic I find professionally familiar."
"I say it the way I'd report a lab value — as data." Most writers never develop a
tic this identifiable. Protect it.

**3. Rhythm control.** Long accumulating sentence, then a short declarative to
land it. "I knew. I'd probably known for a long time." She has an ear.

**4. Structural closure.** She returns to the opening image at the end and lands
it. "The deer have not commented." Rarer than it sounds.

**5. Self-implication.** Her most trustworthy register is the lens turned on
herself — "How I Caught My Own Brain Settling," the borrowed-contempt Note. When
she diagnoses herself, readers trust her. When she diagnoses others, they brace.
**This matters more in cancer writing, not less.**

## Weaknesses — watch for these

**1. The uniqueness escalation.** In "Coming Down the Hill": the number doing
this is small → smaller → "as far as I can tell, one." She felt it go wrong and
disclaimed it immediately ("I'm not saying this to claim uniqueness as a
credential"). When a move needs disclaiming right after it lands, cut the move.
Especially dangerous in the new direction — cancer readers are acutely sensitive
to anyone positioning themselves.

**2. Repetition overrun.** The Storage Wars / sports cards / 3 AM thrillers list
appears three times, near-verbatim, in one essay. The "nervous system has not
agreed to participate in this research" joke runs twice in the body plus the
signature. Her callback instinct is good; the discipline is to vary or cut the
third use.

*But distinguish the two kinds.* In the same essay, "…is not consistent with any
of this" lands three times across three parallel sentences — deliberate anaphora,
it escalates, it works. Keep that. The test: intentional repetition is
structurally parallel and builds; accidental repetition is a callback reused
because the earlier use was forgotten.

*Measured across the archive, repetition scales almost exactly with length* —
her 1,700-word essay is nearly clean, her 4,200-word essay restates its own
mechanism four separate times. Length is the root cause; cutting length fixes
most of it automatically.

**3. The flinch.** She lands a strong ending and then adds one more beat that
softens it. "…started to feel like the thing that was inconsistent. Not the
writing. The silence." — perfect. Then: "Was I complicit?" Trust the landing;
cut the question.

**4. Length drift, unearned.** 1,180 words in March → 3,000–4,200 by May/June,
and the longest pieces are the most repetitive. The essays got baggier, not more
ambitious. **For cancer readers — exhausted, in waiting rooms, reading on
phones — 1,500–2,000 tight beats 4,000 loose every time.**

**5. Without the vignette, the writing collapses.** "The Geopolitics of
D.A.R.V.O." (582 words) is the proof: no personal grounding, straight to the
claim, and it reads like a Note with a headline. This is encouraging news — it
means the vignette → mechanism → point structure *is* the voice, not scaffolding
around it. Never open cold on the thesis.

**6. Disclaimer stacking.** DARVO carries a mid-essay disclaimer, then the Canary
Journal feature, then a second disclaimer. The furniture crowds out the essay.
The temptation will be far stronger in cancer writing — resist it. One standing
disclaimer, one consistent place.

**7. The "My Canary Journal" appendix.** Appended to 9 essays; same three
questions each time, and the answers mostly restate what the essay just said. In
DARVO it is longer than the essay. Good idea, formulaic execution — either give
it independent life or fold it into the body.

## Register for the pivot

The archive voice is *diagnostic* — it names mechanisms and tells you what is
being done to you. The cancer newsletter needs the same eye in a *companion*
register: same precision, less verdict. The self-implicating essays are the
bridge; write from beside the reader rather than across the desk.
